Blue Rim 5™ • Ove Ironhand • The Shield Maiden

The Living Saga

A Norse navigator. His Shield Maiden. A shallow-draft boat. Thousands of years of voices still speaking from the coastline — if someone will show up with genuine respect and sit still long enough to hear them.

Why

Built to Impress One Standard.

People ask who this expedition is meant to impress. Not the sailing community. Not social media. Not institutions, sponsors, or strangers with opinions about what constitutes a worthy adventure. Three decades of work — on submarines, in boardrooms, on the water, and in the kind of places that reshape how a man sees the world — have produced a value set that doesn’t need external validation. The standard is internal. Always has been.

What that means in practice: the planning is meticulous, the preparation is relentless, and the execution is measured against one question — did we do this right? Not fast. Not impressively. Right. The sea is an honest judge and a poor audience. It doesn’t care about the narrative. It rewards preparation, punishes arrogance, and has no interest in anyone’s personal brand. That suits this expedition perfectly.

The highest loyalty here is to humanity itself — not to a tribe, a nation, a religion, a politics, or an ideology. The mission underneath every mile of this route is the advancement, protection, and survival of human civilization and the natural systems that sustain it. The coastlines are not scenery. The people who live on them are not subjects. They are the point. That is the standard the Shield Maiden signed on to.

This is not performance. It is purpose — built from the inside out, over thirty years, and now pointed at the water by two.
The Spirit of the Water

Aloha Wasn’t Words. It Was Love.

There is a philosophy underneath this expedition that goes deeper than seamanship, deeper than preparation, deeper than any credential or credential holder. It is the same philosophy that Israel Kamakawiwoʻole carried in his voice for thirty-eight years and left in the water when he went home to the Pacific in 1997. Izzy called it aloha. It is not a greeting. It is not a cultural decoration. It is a way of moving through the world — to give when nothing is coming back, to carry kindness like a lantern, to leave a little light along your track.

I play baritone ukulele. It was his music that put the instrument in my hands. I cannot say his name the way it deserves to be said — Hawaiian is still a task beyond me — but because of Izzy, I now carry aloha in my heart across every reef I dive and every coastline I sail. That is what this expedition is actually made of underneath everything else.

“Aloha wasn’t words… it was love.”

The Premise

Two Worlds. One Ocean.

A thousand years ago, Norse navigators set out from Scandinavia in shallow-draft wooden boats with no charts, no compass, and no certainty about what lay over the horizon. They read the water. They watched the birds. They arrived at coastlines no European had ever seen — and when they got there, they sat with the people. They traded. They listened. They carried stories home accurately. That was the job. Not conquest. Not extraction. Listening and carrying it true.

“He has truly wandered who has traveled widely — a man who has been nowhere knows nothing.”
— Hávamál, verse 18 • The Sayings of Odin

Catamaran Dan — sailing as Ove Ironhand, whose Y-DNA bloodline traces through the I1 haplogroup to Thorvald Asvaldsson, Erik the Red, and Leif Erikson — and his Shield Maiden, Cintia, move the same way. Shallow draft. Patient landfall. Deep listening. Shamrocket draws twenty-four inches of water and goes where no keeled vessel follows — up inside the reef, into the mangrove channels, onto the beach itself. Right up to where the people are. Where the knowledge lives. Where the stories are still being kept.

What Blue Rim 5™ is building is not a travel record. It is a saga — the living, accumulating account of what the Bahamas and the Florida and Atlantic coast remember, told by the people who have been keeping the memory for thousands of years, finally heard by two people who understand why memory matters and has the discipline to carry it home without distorting it.

The Coastline

Two Seasons. Generations of Memory.

🇧🇸 The Bahamas — Lucayan • Bahamian • Family Islands

The first people of these islands were the Lucayans, who met Columbus in 1492 and were gone within a generation. What came after them is a people who have lived by the water ever since — conch divers, bonefish guides, and fishing families who know the cuts, the banks, and the tides better than any chart. That knowledge is passed down by doing, not by writing. The only way to hear it is to come back, season after season, and earn the conversation.

🇧🇸 The Exumas — The Winter Home

Great Exuma is the expedition's winter base, and that changes everything. A single visit collects a story. Returning every winter builds a relationship. Every spring, Elizabeth Harbour fills with hand-built wooden sloops for the National Family Island Regatta — a living tradition of Bahamian boatbuilding and racing that grew out of the working boats these islands depended on. The saga of the Exumas will be told by the people who are still keeping it.

🇺🇸 The Florida Keys — Conch • Bahamian Roots

The Keys and the Bahamas share more history than most visitors realize. Many of Key West's early settlers came from the Bahamas, and the islands' wreckers made a living salvaging ships off the Florida reef. Locals still call themselves Conchs. Out past Key West, Fort Jefferson stands alone on the Dry Tortugas. The old families of the Keys carry a memory of this reef line — what it looked like, what lived on it, what has been lost — that no survey ever recorded.

🇺🇸 Georgia & the Carolinas — Gullah Geechee

Along the Sea Islands and the coast from northern Florida into the Carolinas live the Gullah Geechee people, descendants of enslaved West and Central Africans. Their language, their sweetgrass baskets, their hand-knotted cast nets, and their knowledge of the marshes and tidal creeks have survived for centuries. Congress recognized this coast as the Gullah Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor. The expedition comes to listen, not to look.

🇺🇸 Cape Lookout — Down East • The Graveyard of the Atlantic

Off the North Carolina coast lie thousands of wrecks — the Graveyard of the Atlantic. The Down East communities around Harkers Island and Core Sound have built boats, fished these waters, and watched these shoals for generations. Their stories of storms, rescues, and wrecks are the human record of the most dangerous stretch of the summer route — and the northern end of the expedition's range.

The Mission

What Blue Rim 5™ Is Actually Doing

Blue Rim 5™ is a moving oral history project and reef awareness platform built into the same two-season rhythm — a sailing expedition that takes its philosophical mandate from the Norse tradition of the skald: go out, sit with the people who live closest to the water, listen to what they know, and carry it home accurately. Then come back next year and listen again.

What gets collected belongs first to the people who gave it. What gets shared with the world gets shared on their terms, with their consent, with their names attached and their community’s context intact. Blue Rim 5™ is not an extraction operation. It is an amplification operation — ensuring that the knowledge, the stories, and the urgent ecological warnings held by these coastal communities reach an audience large enough to matter.

Genuine respect given and received is the currency that opens every door on this coastline. It travels ahead of you. By year three, people on that coastline will be waiting for you to come back — not because you brought equipment, but because you showed up as human beings who saw them clearly and treated what they know as valuable.

I — Go There

Shamrocket’s 24-inch draft gets inside the reef, into the mangrove channels, onto the beach. Where the stories live. Where no research vessel follows.

II — Sit Still

The Norse waited out weather. Blue Rim 5™ waits for trust. Neither is rushed. Both are earned. You cannot collect what you haven’t been invited to receive.

III — Listen Correctly

What sounds like legend is often ecological data. What sounds like myth is often history. What sounds like a fishing story is often the most accurate reef science on the coastline.

IV — Carry It True

The skald’s job was accuracy, not entertainment. The story travels as it was given — with the name of the person who told it, the place it came from, and the community it belongs to.
