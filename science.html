Scientific Methodology

Scientific Foundation. Proven Systems.

The Rim Run™ is supported by Catamaran Dan's Mobile Ocean Observation Platform (MOOP)—a purpose-built, shallow draft (24 inches draft) Hobie Getaway catamaran research vessel designed for continuous, year-round, in-water reef observation and data collection. All survey work is conducted using established, scientifically recognized processes and protocols drawn from the Reef Life Survey (RLS) methodology framework, the REEF survey and species identification resources, and the Reef Check tropical monitoring protocols.

These combined resources provide a disciplined, structured foundation for data collection, helping ensure that observations are consistent, repeatable, and scientifically defensible across locations and over time. The objective is to apply proven methodologies with rigor and precision—producing a long-term, comparable record of reef conditions throughout the Western Caribbean.

This is a longitudinal monitoring system—returning to the same reef sites year after year to produce time-series data where little to none currently exists, aligned with regional scientific priorities where possible.

Survey Execution & Data Integrity

All field operations are executed using disciplined, repeatable survey procedures derived from established reef monitoring systems. Standardized transect methods form the backbone of data collection, with consistent survey geometry, defined observation zones, and controlled swim patterns to ensure comparability across sites and over time.

Surveys are conducted through direct, in-water observation, focusing on fish populations, key invertebrate species, and benthic substrate composition. Each survey follows a structured sequence to maintain spatial consistency, reduce observer bias, and ensure that data collected at different locations and time intervals remains directly comparable.

Data is recorded in real time using standardized logging formats and is subject to continuous internal validation through repetition, cross-checking, and consistent application of methodology. The emphasis is on procedural discipline—ensuring that every dataset contributes to a reliable, long-term record of reef condition throughout the Western Caribbean.

This program is designed for annual repetition along the same route, creating a continuously expanding multi-year dataset rather than a single expedition snapshot.

Field Methodology

This is not casual observation. This is structured, repeatable field data collection conducted over multiple years across the same reef systems. The objective is to maintain consistency, sustainability, and methodological discipline while building a practical observational record that can grow in value over time.

The mission is designed around a core operational reality: the baseline observation program must be able to continue year after year using a disciplined, low-friction field routine. That continuity is the strength of the system. When support, sponsorship, or donations are available, the program can expand into additional measurements, increased site frequency, and more advanced testing.

Measurement Parameters

Priority is consistency over complexity — repeatable data has more long-term value than isolated precision measurements.

Each observation event is structured around a consistent core dataset intended to support repeatability across sites and seasons. Depending on conditions, support, and available equipment, field observations may include:

🌡

Water Conditions

Surface air temperature, subsurface temperature, visibility (Secchi depth), salinity, pH, and ORP (oxidation-reduction potential) — measured at every site using standardized instruments and methods.

🪸

Reef Structure

Reef type classification, substrate composition, and structural complexity — documenting the physical character and integrity of each site.

🔬

Coral Health

Live coral cover, bleaching extent, algae presence, disease indicators, and visible physical damage. Condition recorded across practical field categories: healthy, stressed, bleached, recently dead, or structurally damaged.

🐠

Biological Observations

Indicator fish species, key invertebrates, and invasive species — recorded as repeatable field estimates of abundance and diversity within the observation zone.

⚓

Human Impact

Fishing activity, anchor damage, debris, and vessel traffic — documenting anthropogenic pressures present at each site as part of the standard observation record.

📹

Visual Documentation

Standardized wide-angle, close-up, and transect-style video and photography at every site, preserving a visual record for annual comparison and independent validation.

The emphasis throughout is on consistency of method across sites and over time. Both surface air temperature and subsurface temperature are recorded at every site — the differential between the two provides useful contextual data on thermal stress conditions at the reef surface. Human impact observations are treated as a standard field category, not an afterthought — documenting what is present at each site regardless of whether the reef itself shows damage. This creates a complete contextual record rather than an isolated biological snapshot. Thermocline depth profiling is an expansion measurement added when a depth profiler is available through partner or sponsor support.

Standard Observation Routine

Every reef stop follows a consistent field sequence. A standardized routine reduces procedural drift and ensures that future comparisons remain meaningful:

Step Action Purpose
1 Anchor and stabilize vessel position Establish repeatable site reference and safe working condition
2 Record GPS location and site identity Preserve location accuracy for future annual return
3 Enter water and establish observation depth Create consistent physical survey context
4 Record surface air temperature, subsurface temperature, pH, salinity, ORP, and Secchi visibility Capture full baseline environmental context — all six measurements taken at every site
5 Conduct structured visual scan Assess coral condition, fish activity, substrate, and visible change
6 Capture photo and video documentation Preserve visual record for comparison and validation
7 Exit water and complete written log Lock in observations while details are fresh

SCUBA & Dive-Based Observation

Snorkel-based observation is the operational baseline — it is repeatable, low-friction, and deployable at every site regardless of conditions. But the program's observational reach extends below the surface wherever dive conditions allow. SCUBA capability adds a fundamentally different dimension to the data record: reef walls, deeper coral structure, fish communities at depth, and benthic composition that is simply not accessible from the surface.

Reef Check EcoDiver certification is scheduled for late August 2026 in Bocas del Toro, Panama — directly before the October 1 expedition launch. EcoDiver training establishes structured, scientifically recognized SCUBA survey methods specifically designed for reef condition assessment. Upon certification, SCUBA-based observations at selected sites throughout the circuit will follow Reef Check EcoDiver protocols, making the data directly compatible with Reef Check's global monitoring database.

What SCUBA Adds to the Record

Reef wall documentation — vertical reef structure, deep coral colonies, and sponge communities that define the biological character of a site but are invisible from the surface.

Deeper fish community surveys — species and abundance at depth, including commercially important and ecologically significant species that use the water column rather than the reef flat.

Benthic composition at depth — substrate type, live coral cover, and structural condition at 10–20 meters where conditions can differ significantly from what is observed at snorkel depth.

Bleaching and damage assessment — direct observation of thermal stress, physical damage, and disease at depths where bleaching events often progress differently than at the surface.

The catamaran platform is particularly well-suited to dive operations in this region. A 24-inch draft hull can anchor directly over shallow reef structures that dive boats cannot safely approach — placing the dive platform on top of sites that rarely see in-water observation. Every dive conducted is logged against the same site record as the snorkel survey, building a layered, multi-depth observation record at each location over time.

Equipment

The equipment philosophy is intentionally practical. The baseline system relies on tools that can be sustained operationally over many years. Advanced equipment may be added when external support allows, but the mission does not depend on complex instrumentation in order to remain active.

Baseline Field Tools

Apera PCO60-Z multiparameter meter (surface air temp, subsurface temp, pH, salinity, ORP), DIY Secchi disk (visibility), underwater camera, GPS-enabled device, mask, snorkel, fins, SCUBA equipment, and standardized logging format.

Priority Expansion — Dissolved Oxygen

Dissolved oxygen (DO) is the highest-priority expansion measurement for this program. Hypoxic events in the enclosed bay and lagoon systems of the Western Caribbean — documented in Bocas del Toro, the Belizean coast, and Honduras — have caused mass coral mortality that is frequently misattributed to temperature stress alone because DO goes unmeasured. A longitudinal DO record across 100+ sites would fill a data gap that no existing monitoring program currently addresses. Optical DO sensor added when instrument support is secured.

Further Expansion Tools

PAR light meter (underwater photosynthetically active radiation), depth profiler (thermocline detection), water sampling kit, and additional instrumentation added as partner or sponsor support allows.

Operational Principle

The mission continues with the baseline kit. Support expands what can be measured, but does not determine whether the mission exists.

Data Management

All observations are organized into a structured data system built for long-term usability. A master site index maintains a permanent record of every surveyed location, with each site record containing its own measurement logs, categorized imagery, video documentation, and summary notes. This structure ensures that observations from different years, seasons, and team members can be compared cleanly against a consistent site reference.

Data is indexed and organized to support direct comparison across time and location. The system is designed for an observation program that runs for decades, not a single expedition — so the architecture prioritizes durability, clarity, and accessibility over time.

Data Access & Collaboration

Collected data will be made available in a controlled-access format to support research collaboration, independent analysis, and institutional partnership. Researchers, conservation organizations, and regional reef managers are invited to provide input on measurement priorities, methodologies, and data structures — both prior to expedition launch and during active operation.

The goal is to make this program as useful as possible to the people doing reef science in this region. If there are specific measurements, site priorities, or data formats that would make this record more valuable to existing research efforts, that input is actively sought.

The institutional structure for cross-border data sharing is the Caribbean Coastal Reef Alliance (CCRA) — a seven-nation consortium founded by the Rim Run™ expedition and built to coordinate reef monitoring data across Mexico, Belize, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, and Panama under shared standards. The CCRA provides the framework through which observations collected at any point on the circuit can be accessed, compared, and acted upon by partner governments, scientific institutions, and conservation organizations across the full arc.

Citizen scientists are part of the long-term strength of the system. Their value lies not in improvisation, but in disciplined participation. When trained and aligned to the same routine, citizen scientists can assist with observation, recordkeeping, imagery collection, and repeat visits when the expedition platform is not present.

The objective is to widen the geographic and temporal reach of the program without sacrificing consistency. Standardized method comes first. Simplicity, repeatability, and transparency matter more than overcomplication.

Zone 1 — Northern Arc

Mesoamerican Barrier Reef System

The Western Hemisphere's longest barrier reef at 700 miles. Spans Mexico's Yucatán through Belize, Guatemala, and Honduras. Most studied zone on the circuit — but survey coverage has been dormant for years in key locations.

🇲🇽 Mexico • 🇧🇿 Belize • 🇬🇹 Guatemala • 🇭🇳 Honduras

Extended Caribbean Coastal Ecosystem

Fringing reefs, patch reefs, seagrass beds, and mangrove systems that are even less studied. In Bocas del Toro alone, a 2020 bleaching event impacted 90% of analyzed coral — resulting in 50% loss. Almost no longitudinal monitoring exists.

Data Use & Impact

Who This Serves. Why It Matters.

The Rim Run™ monitoring system is designed to produce usable field observations that can support scientists, conservation organizations, reef managers, educators, local stakeholders, and future collaborative research efforts throughout the Western Caribbean. The goal is not simply to collect information, but to generate consistent, repeat-site, year-over-year records that help document reef condition where long-term observational continuity is limited or absent.

This work is intended to strengthen situational awareness at the local and regional level by contributing direct observations of fish communities, reef condition, benthic composition, and visible ecosystem change across multiple jurisdictions and habitat types. When maintained over time, these records can help identify trends, support comparative analysis, guide conservation discussion, and provide a practical field reference for organizations already working within the region.

Just as important, the project is structured to make disciplined field methods visible and understandable to others. By operating transparently, documenting methods clearly, and returning to the same reef environments repeatedly, the Rim Run™ can help encourage broader participation in defensible reef observation, citizen science, and regional stewardship without sacrificing methodological seriousness.

In that sense, the value of the program is twofold: it produces a growing longitudinal record of reef conditions, and it helps expand the number of people, communities, and institutions that can understand, trust, and make practical use of what that record shows.

The intent is not to replace existing research efforts, but to extend observational reach and continuity across regions where consistent field presence is limited.
