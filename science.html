Scientific Methodology

Scientific Foundation. Proven Systems.

The Rim Run™ is GROAN Level 1 — the field intelligence layer of the Global Reef & Ocean Analytics Network, a five-level ocean decision system developed independently over 18 months as the foundation for the expedition's research. The Mobile Ocean Observation Platform (MOOP) — Shamrocket, a purpose-built Hobie Getaway catamaran with a 24-inch shallow draft — is GROAN's physical decision arm: every deployment is a decision-driven data acquisition act, tasked by the system's intelligence layers to collect field observations that feed directly into cross-ecosystem causal analysis. In the field, this means structured, systematic reef observation across the Caribbean and South Atlantic reef systems. In the system, it means every data point changes a decision.

All survey work is conducted using established, scientifically recognized processes and protocols drawn from the Reef Life Survey (RLS) methodology framework, the REEF survey and species identification resources, and the Reef Check tropical monitoring protocols.

The purpose of this initiative is to build a long-duration, forty-country reef observation system using the Rim Run™ expedition as a consistent field platform.

This field platform is the empirical foundation for high-level research on GROAN — Global Reef & Ocean Analytics Network. The intent is to prove that cross-ecosystem causal intelligence, produced by GROAN's Cross-Module Interaction Engine, enables decisions about ocean systems that single-ecosystem monitoring frameworks are structurally incapable of generating. The Rim Run™ is GROAN's first-person field validation layer: a deliberate, systematic investigation of the gap between what a global ocean decision system requires from its data inputs and what is operationally possible in the real world. Everything above it converts field observations into decisions that single-ecosystem monitoring cannot produce. Reef Research — Full System Overview →

The goal is to establish an operational baseline data framework, collecting structured observations, and building a cross-ecosystem baseline record of reef conditions across a broad set of often undermonitored regions.

This work is being developed within DMAP-CAL™ (Decision Modeling, Analysis, and Probabilistic Control — Adaptive Logic) — GROAN's formal 22-segment mathematical decision engine, which operates through a seven-step operational framework (Define → Measure → Analyze → Predict → Choose → Act → Learn). The system integrates standardized field data with predictive analytics to evaluate potential interventions, assign probability-based outcomes, and produce decisions that are traceable from field observation through causal inference to recommended action. This work will be carried out through standardized in-water surveys, visual documentation, environmental measurements, site-based logging, and disciplined future site monitoring.

These combined resources provide a disciplined, structured foundation for data collection, helping ensure that observations are consistent, repeatable, and scientifically defensible across locations and over time. The objective is to apply proven methodologies with rigor and precision—producing a long-term, comparable record of reef conditions throughout the Caribbean and South Atlantic.

Mission Direction & Intended Outcome

At its core, this program is designed to unite field science, operational discipline, and long-range systems thinking. It is meant to generate more than site notes or isolated observations. It is intended to produce a high quality monitoring architecture, a growing regional data record, a disciplined citizen science support structure, and a future research platform capable of contributing to both practical reef stewardship and formal academic inquiry.

Survey Execution & Data Integrity

All field operations are executed using disciplined, repeatable survey procedures derived from established reef monitoring systems. Standardized transect methods form the backbone of data collection, with consistent survey geometry, defined observation zones, and controlled swim patterns to ensure comparability across sites and conditions.

Surveys are conducted through direct, in-water observation, focusing on fish populations, key invertebrate species, and benthic substrate composition. Each survey follows a structured sequence to maintain spatial consistency, reduce observer bias, and ensure that data collected at different locations and time intervals remains directly comparable.

Data is recorded in real time using standardized logging formats and is subject to continuous internal validation through repetition, cross-checking, and consistent application of methodology. The emphasis is on procedural discipline—ensuring that every dataset contributes to a reliable, long-term record of reef condition throughout the Caribbean and South Atlantic Reef Systems.

01 — BLEACHING PREDICTION

Early Warning 4–12 Weeks Out

GROAN generates bleaching probability forecasts using Degree Heating Week accumulation curves, SST anomaly trajectories, and theater-calibrated historical patterns. Alert tiers — Watch, Warning, Alert Level 1, Alert Level 2 — are issued at site, zone, and theater level, calibrated against NOAA Coral Reef Watch thresholds.

02 — CAUSAL ATTRIBUTION

Why Is the Reef Stressed?

When a site scores poorly, GROAN isolates the dominant stressor and traces it to its origin — an agricultural runoff pulse tracked from Chlorophyll-a spike to DHW accumulation to Decision Score decline. Compound event detection identifies simultaneous stressors that interact multiplicatively, the condition most likely to produce mortality.

03 — FIELD EXPEDITION INTELLIGENCE

Built for At-Sea Operations

Real-time Decision Score and 7-day forecast for any waypoint. Optimal dive window synthesis across wave height, SST, visibility proxy, and current direction. Survey prioritization ranks candidate sites by intelligence value — flagging anomalous scores, recent stress events, and data gaps requiring field verification.

04 — IMAGE INTELLIGENCE (GSIN)

Field Photos → Platform Data in 15 Minutes

Underwater images submitted from the field are processed through the Global Sensor & Image Network, assigned a confidence tier, health-classified by computer vision, and ingested into the decision architecture — closing the loop from field observation to platform intelligence within 5–15 minutes of submission.

05 — CITIZEN SCIENCE BACKBONE

Caribbean Coastal Reef Alliance Integration

GROAN is the processing backbone for the CCRA — a distributed citizen science network operating across the Mesoamerican Reef and wider Caribbean. Volunteer-submitted observations are QC'd against GROAN's confidence tier schema, validated against satellite-derived conditions, and incorporated into the Decision Score with appropriate weighting.

06 — RESEARCH-GRADE DATA OUTPUT

Publication-Ready for Institutional Partners

Normalized multi-source datasets, time-series trend exports, anomaly event archives, and theater-level health reports — all available to credentialed partners via the GROAN API. Cross-theater comparison datasets enable researchers to examine how the same global forcing produces different outcomes across all four theater systems.

07 — AUTOMATED ALERT SYSTEM

Continuous Threshold Monitoring

GROAN monitors all active theater locations continuously — bleaching alerts tiered by CRW classification, runoff and pollution pulse detection, fishing pressure anomalies via Global Fishing Watch integration, and cascade risk alerts when upstream conditions are forecast to produce downstream reef impact within the predictive window.

08 — POLICY & MANAGEMENT SUPPORT

Intelligence at the Institutional Level

MPA effectiveness assessment — Decision Score trends inside vs. outside protection boundaries, quantifying protection value over time. Restoration site prioritization. Climate scenario modeling under IPCC SSP pathways. Fishing pressure data mapped to regulatory boundaries as an evidence base for enforcement.

GROAN is not a data dashboard. Every system that monitors reefs today does so within a single ecosystem. GROAN operates across four simultaneously — computing the causal relationships between them that single-theater platforms are structurally incapable of detecting. When a thermal anomaly in the Red Sea echoes the same ENSO forcing driving bleaching risk on the Mesoamerican Reef, GROAN sees it. That cross-ecosystem causal intelligence is the capability that changes what reef science can deliver — and it is available now.

Field Methodology

Measurement Parameters

Priority is consistency over complexity — repeatable data has more long-term value than isolated precision measurements.

Each observation event is structured around a consistent core dataset intended to support consistency across sites and environmental conditions. Depending on conditions, support, and available equipment, field observations may include:

🌡

Water Conditions

Surface air temperature, subsurface temperature, visibility (Secchi depth), salinity, pH, and ORP (oxidation-reduction potential) — measured at every site using standardized instruments and methods.

🪸

Reef Structure

Reef type classification, substrate composition, and structural complexity — documenting the physical character and integrity of each site.

🔬

Coral Health

Live coral cover, bleaching extent, algae presence, disease indicators, and visible physical damage. Condition recorded across practical field categories: healthy, stressed, bleached, recently dead, or structurally damaged.

🐠

Biological Observations

Indicator fish species, key invertebrates, and invasive species — recorded as repeatable field estimates of abundance and diversity within the observation zone.

⚓

Human Impact

Fishing activity, anchor damage, debris, and vessel traffic — documenting anthropogenic pressures present at each site as part of the standard observation record.

📹

Visual Documentation

Standardized wide-angle, close-up, and transect-style video and photography at every site, preserving a visual record for annual comparison and independent validation.

The emphasis throughout is on consistency of method across sites and over time. Both surface air temperature and subsurface temperature are recorded at every site — the differential between the two provides useful contextual data on thermal stress conditions at the reef surface. Human impact observations are treated as a standard field category, not an afterthought — documenting what is present at each site regardless of whether the reef itself shows damage. This creates a complete contextual record rather than an isolated biological snapshot. Thermocline depth profiling is an expansion measurement added when a depth profiler is available through partner or sponsor support.

Standard Observation Routine

Every reef stop follows a consistent field sequence. A standardized routine reduces procedural drift and ensures that future comparisons remain meaningful:

Step Action Purpose
1 Anchor and stabilize vessel position Establish repeatable site reference and safe working condition
2 Record GPS location and site identity Preserve precise location reference for spatial comparison
3 Enter water and establish observation depth Create consistent physical survey context
4 Record surface air temperature, subsurface temperature, pH, salinity, ORP, and Secchi visibility Capture full baseline environmental context — all six measurements taken at every site
5 Conduct structured visual scan Assess coral condition, fish activity, substrate, and visible change
6 Capture photo and video documentation Preserve visual record for comparison and validation
7 Exit water and complete written log Lock in observations while details are fresh

SCUBA & Dive-Based Observation

Snorkel-based observation is the operational baseline — it is repeatable, low-friction, and deployable at every site regardless of conditions. But the program's observational reach extends below the surface wherever dive conditions allow. SCUBA capability adds a fundamentally different dimension to the data record: reef walls, deeper coral structure, fish communities at depth, and benthic composition that is simply not accessible from the surface.

What SCUBA Adds to the Record

Reef wall documentation — vertical reef structure, deep coral colonies, and sponge communities that define the biological character of a site but are invisible from the surface.

Deeper fish community surveys — species and abundance at depth, including commercially important and ecologically significant species that use the water column rather than the reef flat.

Benthic composition at depth — substrate type, live coral cover, and structural condition at 10–20 meters where conditions can differ significantly from what is observed at snorkel depth.

Bleaching and damage assessment — direct observation of thermal stress, physical damage, and disease at depths where bleaching events often progress differently than at the surface.

The catamaran platform is particularly well-suited to dive operations in this region. A 24-inch draft hull can anchor directly over shallow reef structures that dive boats cannot safely approach — placing the dive platform on top of sites that rarely see in-water observation. Every dive conducted is logged against the same site record as the snorkel survey, building a layered, multi-depth observation record at each location over time.

Equipment

The equipment philosophy is intentionally practical. The baseline system relies on tools that can be sustained operationally over many years. Advanced equipment may be added when external support allows, but the mission does not depend on complex instrumentation in order to remain active.

Baseline Field Tools

Apera PCO60-Z multiparameter meter (surface air temp, subsurface temp, pH, salinity, ORP), DIY Secchi disk (visibility), underwater camera, GPS-enabled device, mask, snorkel, fins, SCUBA equipment, and standardized logging format.

Priority Expansion — Dissolved Oxygen

Dissolved oxygen (DO) is the highest-priority expansion measurement for this program. Hypoxic events in the enclosed bay and lagoon systems of the Western Caribbean — documented in Bocas del Toro, the Belizean coast, and Honduras — have caused mass coral mortality that is frequently misattributed to temperature stress alone because DO goes unmeasured. A longitudinal DO record across 100+ sites would fill a data gap that no existing monitoring program currently addresses. Optical DO sensor added when instrument support is secured.

Further Expansion Tools

PAR light meter (underwater photosynthetically active radiation), depth profiler (thermocline detection), water sampling kit, and additional instrumentation added as partner or sponsor support allows.

Operational Principle

The mission continues with the baseline kit. Support expands what can be measured, but does not determine whether the mission exists.

Data Management

All observations are organized into a structured data system built for long-term usability. A master site index maintains a permanent record of every surveyed location, with each site record containing its own measurement logs, categorized imagery, video documentation, and summary notes. This structure ensures that observations across the expedition timeline, seasons, and team members can be compared cleanly against a consistent site reference.

Data is indexed and organized to support direct comparison across time and location. The system is designed for an observation program that runs for decades, not a single expedition — so the architecture prioritizes durability, clarity, and accessibility over time.

Data Access & Collaboration

Collected data will be made available in a controlled-access format to support research collaboration, independent analysis, and institutional partnership. Researchers, conservation organizations, and regional reef managers are invited to provide input on measurement priorities, methodologies, and data structures — both prior to expedition launch and during active operation.

The goal is to make this program as useful as possible to the people doing reef science in this region. If there are specific measurements, site priorities, or data formats that would make this record more valuable to existing research efforts, that input is actively sought.

Citizen scientists are part of the long-term strength of the system. Their value lies not in improvisation, but in disciplined participation. When trained and aligned to the same routine, citizen scientists can assist with observation, recordkeeping, imagery collection, and additional observations when the expedition platform is not present.

The objective is to widen the geographic and temporal reach of the program without sacrificing consistency. Standardized method comes first. Simplicity, repeatability, and transparency matter more than overcomplication.

Data Use & Impact

Who This Serves. Why It Matters.

The Rim Run™ monitoring system is designed to produce usable field observations that can support scientists, conservation organizations, reef managers, educators, local stakeholders, and future collaborative research efforts throughout the Western Caribbean. The goal is not simply to collect information, but to generate consistent, repeat-site, cross-region comparative records that help document reef condition where long-term observational continuity is limited or absent.

This work is intended to strengthen situational awareness at the local and regional level by contributing direct observations of fish communities, reef condition, benthic composition, and visible ecosystem change across multiple jurisdictions and habitat types. When maintained over time, these records can help identify trends, support comparative analysis, guide conservation discussion, and provide a practical field reference for organizations already working within the region.

Just as important, the project is structured to make disciplined field methods visible and understandable to others. By operating transparently, documenting methods clearly, and returning to the same reef environments repeatedly, the Rim Run™ can help encourage broader participation in defensible reef observation, citizen science, and regional stewardship without sacrificing methodological seriousness.

In that sense, the value of the program is twofold: it produces a growing longitudinal record of reef conditions, and it helps expand the number of people, communities, and institutions that can understand, trust, and make practical use of what that record shows.

The long-term results sought are clear: a scientifically useful multi-year reef dataset; a practical and repeatable marine monitoring architecture; a disciplined citizen science network that can widen geographic reach without abandoning rigor; and a body of field-based knowledge capable of supporting cross-border collaboration, institutional research partnerships, and better-informed reef stewardship throughout the region.

The intent is not to replace existing research efforts, but to extend observational reach and continuity across regions where consistent field presence is limited.
