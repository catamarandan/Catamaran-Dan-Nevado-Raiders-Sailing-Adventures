▸ Lecture — Biological Oceanography: Dynamics
🇺🇸 English

Processes (L04) describe what the reef system does at a given moment. Dynamics describe how the system changes over time — how populations grow, crash, and recover; how communities shift between alternative stable states; how disturbances interact with resilience to determine whether a reef returns to its pre-disturbance condition or locks into a degraded alternative. Understanding dynamics is what separates a monitoring system that reports what happened from a decision system that predicts what will happen next. GROAN's CMIE is a dynamics engine, not a status dashboard.

Population Dynamics describe the change in abundance, age structure, and spatial distribution of a species through time, governed by birth rates, death rates, immigration, and emigration. In reef systems the most consequential population dynamic is the coral cover trajectory — the rate of change of live coral cover per unit time, positive (recovery) or negative (degradation). A single coral cover measurement tells you where a reef is; the cover trajectory tells you where it is going. GROAN's CMIE requires at least two temporally separated GRIN observations at a site before it can produce a trajectory-based Decision Score — a single observation produces a status score only. The Rim Run™ annual circuit is designed precisely to generate the time series that enables trajectory scoring from circuit 2 onward.

Phase Shifts are abrupt, non-linear transitions between alternative stable states — the most consequential dynamic in reef science. The canonical reef phase shift is coral-dominated state → macroalgae-dominated state, driven by the combination of coral mortality (removing the dominant space-holder) and removal of herbivores (releasing macroalgae from grazing control). Once established, the macroalgae state is self-reinforcing: algae suppress coral recruitment, occupy substrate, and maintain the shift against moderate disturbance. Phase shifts exhibit hysteresis — the stressor level required to reverse a phase shift is substantially lower than the level that caused it. This asymmetry is the defining challenge of reef management: preventing a phase shift requires far less intervention than reversing one after it has occurred. GROAN's Decision Score weights this asymmetry explicitly — a site approaching the tipping threshold receives a higher urgency score than its current status metrics alone would justify.

Resilience and Resistance are two distinct dynamic properties that are frequently conflated. Resistance is the ability of a system to avoid being displaced from its current state by a disturbance — measured as the magnitude of disturbance required to cause a state change. Resilience is the ability of a system to return to its pre-disturbance state after displacement — measured by the rate and completeness of recovery. A reef can be resistant but not resilient (survives bleaching without phase shift, but recovers extremely slowly), or resilient but not resistant (shifts to algae state easily, but recovers quickly when stressors are removed). GROAN's CMIE tracks both properties separately: resistance is estimated from the magnitude of bleaching events survived without phase shift; resilience is estimated from the recovery trajectory rate between disturbance events. These two indices produce different DMAP-CAL™ routing decisions.

Pulse vs. Press Disturbances are the two fundamental disturbance categories with radically different management implications. A pulse disturbance is acute, short-duration, and bounded in time — a bleaching event, a hurricane, a crown-of-thorns outbreak. The system is displaced abruptly and then the disturbance ends, allowing recovery if resilience is sufficient. A press disturbance is chronic, continuous, and sustained — elevated nutrient loading, chronic sedimentation, persistent overfishing. Press disturbances erode resilience over time without causing acute state changes, making them invisible to monitoring systems that only detect acute events. The MAR corridor is under simultaneous pulse (bleaching events) and press (chronic land-based pollution, overfishing) disturbance — a compound disturbance regime that GROAN's cross-module CMIE is uniquely structured to detect. A monitoring system that responds only to pulse events will systematically underestimate the cumulative stressor load at sites where press disturbances are the dominant driver.

Metapopulation Dynamics and Connectivity describe reef systems not as isolated populations but as networks of semi-independent local populations connected by larval dispersal. A local reef population can go extinct and be recolonized if a source population within dispersal range persists. Source reefs — those producing more larvae than they consume — are the nodes that sustain the entire network. Sink reefs — those dependent on external larval supply — can appear healthy while remaining demographically dependent on distant source populations. Identifying source-sink structure across the MAR corridor is one of GROAN's highest-value long-term outputs: a DMAP-CAL™ Decision Score that prioritizes protection of source reefs produces corridor-wide network resilience, while a Decision Score that responds only to local site condition may allow source reefs to degrade while protecting sinks that cannot sustain themselves without that source.

Non-linear Regime Dynamics and Early Warning Signals are the frontier of reef dynamics science. As a system approaches a tipping point, it exhibits characteristic mathematical signatures: critical slowing down (the system takes longer to return to equilibrium after small perturbations — detectable as increased autocorrelation in time series), increasing variance (fluctuations around the stable state grow larger as the attractor weakens), and flickering (transient excursions toward the alternative state before the permanent shift). GROAN's 20-year corridor time series is specifically designed to generate the temporal resolution required to detect critical slowing down at individual reef sites — a capability that requires a minimum of 5–7 annual observations before the statistical signal becomes distinguishable from noise. No single expedition or fixed-station network can produce this signal at corridor scale. The Rim Run™ can.

Dinámica de Poblaciones: El CMIE de GROAN requiere al menos dos observaciones GRIN temporalmente separadas en un sitio antes de poder producir una Puntuación de Decisión basada en trayectoria — una sola observación produce solo una puntuación de estado. Cambios de Fase: El cambio de fase canónico en arrecifes es estado dominado por coral → estado dominado por macroalgas. Los cambios de fase exhiben histéresis — el nivel de estresor requerido para revertir un cambio de fase es sustancialmente menor que el nivel que lo causó. Prevenir un cambio de fase requiere mucho menos intervención que revertirlo después de que ha ocurrido.

Resiliencia y Resistencia: La resistencia es la capacidad de evitar el desplazamiento del estado actual; la resiliencia es la capacidad de regresar al estado previo a la perturbación. Un arrecife puede ser resistente pero no resiliente, o resiliente pero no resistente. El CMIE de GROAN rastrea ambas propiedades por separado, produciendo decisiones de enrutamiento de DMAP-CAL™ diferentes.

Perturbaciones de Pulso vs. Presión: Una perturbación de pulso es aguda y acotada en el tiempo — un evento de blanqueamiento, un huracán. Una perturbación de presión es crónica y sostenida — carga elevada de nutrientes, sobrepesca persistente. El corredor MAR está bajo un régimen de perturbación compuesta simultánea de pulso y presión que el CMIE de GROAN está estructurado de manera única para detectar. Un sistema de monitoreo que solo responde a eventos de pulso subestimará sistemáticamente la carga acumulada de estresores.

Metapoblación y Conectividad: Los arrecifes fuente — aquellos que producen más larvas de las que consumen — son los nodos que sostienen toda la red. Una Puntuación de Decisión de DMAP-CAL™ que prioriza la protección de arrecifes fuente produce resiliencia de red en todo el corredor.

Dinámica de Régimen No Lineal: La serie temporal de 20 años del corredor de GROAN está diseñada para generar la resolución temporal necesaria para detectar el critical slowing down en sitios arrecifales individuales — una capacidad que requiere un mínimo de 5–7 observaciones anuales antes de que la señal estadística sea distinguible del ruido.

▸ Application — GROAN Dynamics Diagnostics & CMIE Routing
🇺🇸 English
  • When recording GRIN belt transect data at a corridor site, always record the current observation against all prior circuit data before assigning a Decision Score category. A site with 25% coral cover is not interpretable without its trajectory: 25% cover trending upward from 18% two circuits ago is a recovering site; 25% cover trending downward from 42% two circuits ago is an accelerating degradation site. The DMAP-CAL™ intervention routing for these two sites is opposite — the recovering site receives monitoring continuity and recruitment support; the degrading site receives immediate chronic stressor identification and Tier 2–3 intervention targeting. Status-only scoring without trajectory produces systematically wrong routing decisions.
  • When a corridor site shows macroalgae cover exceeding 40% and herbivore biomass below 30% of baseline, evaluate phase shift proximity before assigning a standard degradation score. If the coral:algae transition has been progressive over more than two circuits with no acute disturbance event driving the change, the site is exhibiting the approach to a phase shift driven by chronic press disturbances — not a post-pulse recovery scenario. Enter a tipping threshold proximity flag in CMIE with an explicit hysteresis note: reversing this trajectory will require stressor reduction to a level substantially below the current threshold, not merely stabilization at current levels.
  • When comparing two corridor sites with similar current coral cover but different disturbance histories, assess resistance and resilience separately before assigning relative priority. Site A with 35% cover that survived a DHW-8 bleaching event two circuits ago without phase shift has demonstrated high resistance — it should receive a higher resilience confidence score than Site B with 35% cover that has never been exposed to significant thermal stress. Site B's untested resilience means its response to the next bleaching event is unknown; Site A's demonstrated resistance means it is a higher-value protection and monitoring investment for the corridor network.
  • When assessing a site that has experienced multiple successive pulse disturbances (two bleaching events within five years, or a bleaching event followed by a hurricane within three years), evaluate whether compound pulse effects are eroding resilience toward a press-equivalent dynamic. Successive pulse disturbances with insufficient recovery time between them function dynamically like a press disturbance — the system never returns to full resilience before the next displacement. Enter this in the CMIE record as a compounding disturbance trajectory, not as two independent pulse events, and weight the Decision Score accordingly with a reduced recovery time discount.
  • From circuit 5 onward, include a critical slowing down assessment in the CMIE record for each site with a continuous 5+ circuit time series: calculate the lag-1 autocorrelation of coral cover values and the variance of cover values across circuits. An increasing autocorrelation trend (values becoming more similar to the prior circuit than expected) combined with increasing variance (cover fluctuating more widely each circuit) is the statistical signature of critical slowing down — the reef's attractor is weakening and it is approaching a tipping threshold. This is the highest-confidence early warning signal available in the GROAN data architecture and should trigger an immediate escalation in DMAP-CAL™ Decision Score priority regardless of current cover level.

QUESTION 01
Two corridor sites both record 28% live coral cover in circuit 4. Site A showed 18% cover in circuit 2 and 23% in circuit 3. Site B showed 45% cover in circuit 2 and 36% in circuit 3. Why does the CMIE produce opposite DMAP-CAL™ routing decisions for these sites despite identical current status?
Dos sitios del corredor registran 28% de cobertura coralina en el circuito 4. El Sitio A mostró 18% en el circuito 2 y 23% en el circuito 3. El Sitio B mostró 45% en el circuito 2 y 36% en el circuito 3. ¿Por qué el CMIE produce decisiones de enrutamiento DMAP-CAL™ opuestas a pesar del estado actual idéntico?
  • A. The sites should receive the same routing — current cover is the only defensible basis for intervention prioritization; trajectory data introduces too much uncertainty
  • B. Site A receives higher priority because lower absolute cover always indicates greater urgency regardless of trend direction
  • C. Site A is on a positive recovery trajectory (+10 pp over two circuits) and receives monitoring continuity and recruitment support; Site B is on an accelerating negative trajectory (−17 pp over two circuits) and requires immediate chronic stressor identification and Tier 2–3 intervention — the trajectory reverses the apparent status ranking entirely and produces opposite management responses
  • D. Both sites require immediate intervention; trajectory data is only used to prioritize between sites with identical intervention needs, not to change the intervention type
ANSWER: C · RESPUESTA: C
QUESTION 02
A corridor site has experienced bleaching events in circuits 1, 3, and 5 — each recovering partially before the next event. The site has never undergone a phase shift and current coral cover remains at 22%. Why should the CMIE enter this as a compounding disturbance trajectory rather than three independent pulse events, and what does this imply for the Decision Score?
Un sitio ha experimentado eventos de blanqueamiento en los circuitos 1, 3 y 5, con recuperación parcial entre cada uno. La cobertura actual es del 22%. ¿Por qué el CMIE debe registrar esto como trayectoria de perturbación compuesta y qué implica para la Puntuación de Decisión?
  • A. Three pulse events are always logged independently — compounding effects are theoretical and not yet supported by field evidence in the MAR corridor
  • B. Successive pulse disturbances with insufficient recovery time between them function dynamically like a press disturbance — the system never returned to full resilience before the next displacement; each event compounds the prior resilience deficit; the CMIE must apply a reduced recovery time discount to the Decision Score, reflecting that the site's resilience baseline is lower than its cover level suggests, and that the next bleaching event will have a higher phase shift probability than a naively status-based DS would indicate
  • C. The site should be reclassified as under press disturbance only — once bleaching events exceed two in five circuits, the pulse classification no longer applies
  • D. 22% cover after three bleaching events indicates higher-than-expected resistance; the DS should reflect a positive resilience assessment, not a compounding disturbance flag
ANSWER: B · RESPUESTA: B
