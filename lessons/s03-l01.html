▸ Lecture — Biological Oceanography: Foundations
🇺🇸 English

Biological oceanography is the study of how living organisms interact with and shape the physical, chemical, and geological properties of the ocean — and how the ocean shapes them in return. It differs fundamentally from marine biology, which focuses primarily on the organisms themselves. The marine biologist asks "what lives here?" The biological oceanographer asks: why does it live here, in these densities, doing these things — and what does that do to the ocean? That question — system function, not organism description — organizes every GROAN module and every field observation protocol aboard Shamrocket.

Ocean organisms are classified by their relationship to the water column — not by taxonomy but by how they live. Plankton (plancton) drift with currents and cannot swim against them. They include phytoplankton — photosynthetic, plant-like organisms that fix carbon at the ocean surface and constitute T1 of every reef food web — and zooplankton — animal-like grazers at T2 that bridge primary production and higher trophic levels. Together they drive the biological carbon pump and are the foundational input to GROAN's GKIN module. Nekton (nectón) are active swimmers — fish, squid, marine mammals, sea turtles — capable of moving independently of currents. They link trophic levels across vast spatial scales and are a primary GRIN observation target. Benthos (bentos) live at or near the seafloor — corals, sponges, echinoderms, seagrasses, crustose coralline algae. Benthic communities are the primary observation target of reef science and of GROAN's GRIN and GSIN field modules. Their condition is what the entire GROAN decision chain exists to protect.

Energy moves through ocean ecosystems in trophic levels governed by one foundational constraint: approximately 10% of energy passes from each trophic level to the next. The remainder is lost to metabolism, respiration, excretion, and decomposition. The consequence is non-linear and severe: a 50% collapse in T1 primary productivity does not produce a 50% decline at T4 — it produces approximately 99.95% energy starvation across three trophic steps. This non-linearity is precisely the class of cascade signal the CMIE is built to detect across modules. A single-module monitoring system cannot see it. GROAN can.

Primary productivity is the rate at which photosynthetic organisms fix carbon from CO₂ into organic matter, measured in mg C m⁻² day⁻¹ or g C m⁻² yr⁻¹. Gross Primary Production (GPP) is total carbon fixed before respiration losses. Net Primary Production (NPP) is what remains available to the food web after the organism's own metabolic costs — the operationally relevant figure for CMIE inputs and reef condition modeling. Reporting GPP where NPP is required overstates reef productive capacity and inflates Decision Scores.

Tropical coral reefs are anomalously productive relative to the nutrient-poor "blue desert" ocean surrounding them. The mechanism is coral-zooxanthellae symbiosis: dinoflagellate algae (Symbiodinium spp.) living within coral tissue photosynthesize and transfer up to 90% of fixed carbon to their coral host, creating a highly efficient internal recycling loop that bypasses dependence on external nutrient supply. Bleaching is the physiological decoupling of this unit — not a population decline event but a systemic NPP collapse that must be entered in the CMIE record as a T1 productivity failure with immediate cascade implications for T2–T5 biomass.

The operationally relevant depth window for all GROAN reef-condition field observations is the neritic photic zone — the upper 30–40 m over continental shelves, where light, temperature, and carbonate saturation together produce the conditions reefs require. Data collected below this window belongs to a mesopelagic ecological context with different species assemblages, nutrient dynamics, and light regimes. Including sub-40 m data in reef-condition trend analysis without explicit depth-context flagging corrupts longitudinal comparisons across Rim Run™ circuits.

La energía se transfiere entre niveles tróficos con una eficiencia de aproximadamente 10% por paso. Esta no linealidad es precisamente la clase de señal de cascada que el CMIE está construido para detectar entre módulos. Un sistema de monitoreo de módulo único no puede verlo. GROAN sí puede.

La NPP — no la GPP — es la figura operacionalmente relevante para el modelado de condición arrecifal y las entradas del CMIE. La simbiosis coral-zooxantelas crea un bucle de reciclaje interno que hace que los arrecifes sean extraordinariamente productivos a pesar del océano pobre en nutrientes que los rodea. El blanqueamiento es el desacople fisiológico de esta unidad — un colapso sistémico de NPP que debe registrarse en el CMIE como falla de productividad T1 con implicaciones inmediatas de cascada para la biomasa T2–T5. Todas las observaciones de campo de GROAN se limitan a la zona fótica nerítica — los 30–40 m superiores sobre la plataforma continental.

▸ Application — GROAN Field Protocol & CMIE Routing
🇺🇸 English
  • When classifying an ocean organism for GROAN field logging, determine ecological category — plankton, nekton, or benthos — before taxonomic group. Category determines observation protocol, data field selection, and module routing. A parrotfish is nekton at T2; it is logged in GRIN under herbivore trophic function. Its taxonomy is secondary to its functional role in the coral-algae competitive balance — the system-level signal GROAN tracks.
  • Apply the 10% Rule when evaluating trophic cascade severity at a corridor site. A 50% measured decline in phytoplankton density at T1 produces approximately 99.95% energy reduction at T4. The CMIE must flag T3–T4 biomass collapse as a leading indicator of the T1 event, not a lagging consequence. Do not wait for fish survey data to confirm cascade effects — the non-linear projection is available the moment the T1 signal is recorded.
  • Use NPP, not GPP, in all CMIE inputs and reef productivity discussions. Reporting GPP where NPP is the correct metric overstates reef carrying capacity and inflates Decision Scores. The distinction matters most during bleaching assessment: GPP may remain partially positive while NPP has collapsed, creating a false-positive reading if the wrong metric is logged.
  • Confine all GROAN reef-condition field observations to the neritic photic zone — upper 30–40 m. If a deeper observation is scientifically warranted, log it separately with a depth-context flag and do not merge it into the standard reef-condition record. A single sub-40 m data point merged into the trend record creates a methodological fault line that propagates through every subsequent circuit comparison at that site.
  • Treat the coral-zooxanthellae symbiosis as a single productive unit in all GROAN modeling. When a bleaching event is detected, enter it in the CMIE record as a systemic T1 NPP collapse signal covering the affected area and immediately initiate cascade projection for T2–T5 biomass implications. Entering it only as a benthic cover metric in GRIN misses the cross-module cascade and produces a Decision Score that does not reflect actual system condition.

▸ Assessment — 3 Questions / Evaluación — 3 Preguntas
QUESTION 01
A researcher studies how a phytoplankton bloom alters dissolved oxygen and carbonate saturation in a reef lagoon. This work is best described as what discipline, and why?
Un investigador estudia cómo una floración de fitoplancton altera el oxígeno disuelto y la saturación de carbonatos en una laguna arrecifal. ¿Cómo se describe mejor este trabajo y por qué?
  • A. Marine biology — because it involves living organisms in the sea
  • B. Chemical oceanography — because it measures dissolved gases and carbonate chemistry
  • C. Biological oceanography — because it examines the bidirectional interaction between organisms and ocean system properties: how the bloom alters ocean chemistry and how that chemistry constrains organism function
  • D. Physical oceanography — because lagoon currents distribute the bloom spatially
ANSWER: C · RESPUESTA: C
QUESTION 02
Primary productivity at a corridor reef site collapses 80% due to a bleaching event. Using the 10% trophic transfer rule, what is the approximate energy available at T4 relative to pre-bleaching baseline, and what does this mean for CMIE cascade projection?
La productividad primaria colapsa un 80% por blanqueamiento. Usando la regla del 10%, ¿cuál es la energía aproximada en T4 relativa a la línea de base previa, y qué significa esto para la proyección de cascada del CMIE?
  • A. ~20% — T4 mirrors the T1 decline proportionally; cascade projection is linear
  • B. ~0.02% — 80% T1 collapse leaves 20% of baseline; three successive 10% transfer steps yield 0.02% of original energy; the CMIE must immediately project effective T4 ecosystem collapse as a leading indicator, not wait for fish survey confirmation
  • C. ~8% — the 10% rule applies once across the full chain, not at each individual step
  • D. ~50% — apex predators buffer T4 by switching prey; the cascade is dampened above T3
ANSWER: B · RESPUESTA: B
QUESTION 03
A MOOP operator detects a significant bleaching event at a corridor site and logs it only as a benthic cover decline in GRIN. What critical error has been made, and what is the correct CMIE entry?
Un operador MOOP detecta un blanqueamiento significativo y lo registra solo como declive de cobertura bentónica en GRIN. ¿Qué error crítico se cometió y cuál es la entrada correcta del CMIE?
  • A. No error — benthic cover is the correct primary field; CMIE effects are inferred automatically
  • B. Should have logged in GSIN because bleaching affects zooxanthellae
  • C. Bleaching was treated as a population event rather than a systemic NPP collapse. The correct CMIE entry is a T1 primary productivity collapse signal for the affected area with immediate cascade projection for T2–T5 biomass — not a benthic cover metric alone
  • D. Must wait for fish biomass survey data before entering any CMIE cross-module flag
ANSWER: C · RESPUESTA: C
