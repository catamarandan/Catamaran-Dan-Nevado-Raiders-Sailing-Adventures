Core Principles

Liebig's Law · Redfield Ratio · Sverdrup Hypothesis · Biological Pump · Diel Vertical Migration · Competitive Exclusion

Lecture
EN — English

Six principles govern the behavior of ocean biological systems with the same weight that carbonate equilibria govern ocean chemistry. A reef scientist who understands trophic structure but cannot apply Liebig's Law of the Minimum, the Redfield Ratio, or the Sverdrup Critical Depth Hypothesis cannot diagnose why productivity at a given site is what it is — and therefore cannot predict how it will respond to stressors. These are not academic principles. They are operational diagnostics. GROAN's module architecture encodes all six.

Principle 01 · Liebig's Law of the Minimum
Limiting nutrient Growth ceiling Non-additive stressors

Formulated by Justus von Liebig (1840), the Law of the Minimum states that biological productivity is controlled not by the total resources available but by whichever essential resource is in shortest supply relative to need — the limiting factor. In ocean systems, this is typically a macronutrient (nitrogen, phosphorus, silicate) or micronutrient (iron in HNLC regions). For GROAN: when a reef site shows depressed NPP despite adequate light and temperature, the diagnostic entry point is Liebig — identify which nutrient is limiting, and the causal pathway clarifies. The principle is also non-additive: adding any non-limiting nutrient produces zero additional growth. This is critical for DMAP-CAL™ intervention routing — an action targeting the wrong limiting factor wastes resources.

Principle 02 · The Redfield Ratio
C:N:P = 106:16:1 Stoichiometric baseline Deviation = stress signal

Alfred Redfield (1934, 1958) observed that the ratio of carbon, nitrogen, and phosphorus in marine phytoplankton biomass — approximately C:N:P = 106:16:1 — closely matches the ratio of these elements in deep ocean seawater. This convergence implies a co-evolutionary coupling between organism stoichiometry and ocean geochemistry maintained over geological timescales. For GROAN: the Redfield Ratio is the stoichiometric baseline against which nutrient water chemistry is interpreted. Seawater deviating significantly from Redfield proportions (elevated N:P = anthropogenic N loading; depressed N:P = N fixation or denitrification pulse) signals a perturbed nutrient regime — an input to both GKIN and GRIN module context. Reef bleaching events frequently alter local N:P ratios through thermal disruption of zooxanthellae N cycling.

Principle 03 · Sverdrup Critical Depth Hypothesis
Mixed layer depth Compensation depth Spring bloom trigger

Harald Sverdrup (1953) proposed that a phytoplankton bloom initiates when the surface mixed layer becomes shallower than a critical depth — the depth at which depth-integrated photosynthesis equals depth-integrated respiration. Above the critical depth, phytoplankton spend enough time in the photic zone to produce net positive growth. Below it, mixing dilutes cells into darkness faster than they can photosynthesize. For GROAN: the Sverdrup Hypothesis explains why the Caribbean spring productivity peak precedes the northern temperate bloom by months — shallow mixed layers driven by warm SST and stratification. It also explains why thermal stratification from SST anomalies suppresses nutrient mixing and reduces NPP — one of the primary mechanisms by which climate warming degrades reef productivity before bleaching temperature thresholds are reached.

Principle 04 · The Biological Carbon Pump
Vertical C export Sequestration flux Pump efficiency

The biological pump is the ocean's primary mechanism for removing CO₂ from the surface and sequestering it in the deep — driven by the sinking of dead organic matter, fecal pellets, and aggregates (marine snow) from the photic zone. Approximately 5–20% of surface productivity is exported below 100 m; of that, roughly 1% reaches the seafloor. The pump's efficiency determines the ocean's capacity as a long-term carbon sink and directly modulates atmospheric CO₂ concentrations on geological timescales. For GROAN: a degraded reef system with reduced calcification and productivity operates a weakened biological pump — with measurable consequences for local Ω aragonite and regional carbon flux. GSIN seagrass meadows within the corridor function as supplementary pump enhancers: their burial efficiency rivals deep-sea sediment traps. Pump degradation is therefore a cross-module CMIE signal, not a single-module measurement.

Principle 05 · Diel Vertical Migration
Predator avoidance Active carbon transport Night ascent

Diel vertical migration (DVM) is the largest daily animal movement on Earth — billions of zooplankton, krill, myctophid fish, and squid ascending hundreds of meters to the surface at night to feed, then descending to depth at dawn to avoid visual predators. DVM creates an active biological pump component: organisms feeding at the surface, respiring, and excreting at depth effectively inject dissolved and particulate carbon below the thermocline. Estimated to contribute 15–40% of total biological carbon export at some sites. For GROAN: DVM patterns shift with thermal anomalies — warming compresses the vertical range available for migration and alters the mesopelagic oxygen minimum zone depth, with consequences for trophic connectivity between reef systems and open-ocean midwater communities that GKIN's kinetic indices must account for.

Principle 06 · Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle and the Paradox of the Plankton
One niche · one species Paradox of the plankton Non-equilibrium coexistence Intermediate disturbance

Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle states that two species competing for identical limiting resources cannot coexist stably — the superior competitor will displace the other. Yet the ocean hosts hundreds of phytoplankton species apparently competing for the same handful of nutrients in the same water column. Hutchinson (1961) named this the Paradox of the Plankton. The resolution lies in non-equilibrium dynamics: physical mixing events, light fluctuations, grazing pressure, and seasonal nutrient pulses continuously reset competitive hierarchies before any single species can exclude others. The Intermediate Disturbance Hypothesis formalizes this: maximum species diversity occurs at intermediate disturbance frequencies — neither complete stasis nor constant disruption. For GROAN: this principle frames reef health interpretation — a reef with reduced disturbance variability (e.g., a thermally stabilized, stratified system under climate change) may paradoxically lose biodiversity even without acute bleaching events. The CMIE must evaluate disturbance regime as a variable, not merely stressor intensity. GRIN's species richness metrics and GKIN's kinetic indices together operationalize this principle at the corridor level.

ES — Español

Seis principios gobiernan el comportamiento de los sistemas biológicos oceánicos con el mismo peso que la equilibria de carbonatos gobierna la química oceánica. Un científico arrecifal que comprende la estructura trófica pero no puede aplicar la Ley del Mínimo de Liebig, la Relación de Redfield o la Hipótesis de Profundidad Crítica de Sverdrup no puede diagnosticar por qué la productividad en un sitio dado es lo que es — y por lo tanto no puede predecir cómo responderá a los factores estresantes.

Ley del Mínimo de Liebig (1840): La productividad biológica está controlada no por el total de recursos disponibles sino por el recurso esencial que se encuentra en menor suministro relativo a la necesidad — el factor limitante. Para GROAN: cuando un sitio arrecifal muestra NPP deprimida a pesar de luz y temperatura adecuadas, el punto de entrada diagnóstica es Liebig. Una acción que apunta al factor limitante incorrecto desperdicia recursos.

Relación de Redfield (1934/1958): La relación de carbono, nitrógeno y fósforo en la biomasa fitoplanctónica marina — aproximadamente C:N:P = 106:16:1 — coincide estrechamente con la relación de estos elementos en el agua marina profunda. Las desviaciones de las proporciones de Redfield señalan un régimen de nutrientes perturbado — una señal de entrada tanto para GKIN como para GRIN.

Hipótesis de Sverdrup (1953): Un florecimiento fitoplanctónico se inicia cuando la capa de mezcla superficial se vuelve más superficial que una profundidad crítica — donde la fotosíntesis integrada en profundidad iguala la respiración integrada en profundidad. Esto explica por qué la estratificación térmica de las anomalías de TSM suprime la mezcla de nutrientes y reduce la NPP antes de que se alcancen los umbrales de temperatura de blanqueamiento.

Bomba Biológica de Carbono: La bomba biológica es el mecanismo primario del océano para eliminar el CO₂ de la superficie y secuestrarlo en las profundidades. Los prados de pastos marinos de GSIN dentro del corredor funcionan como mejoradores suplementarios de la bomba: su eficiencia de enterramiento rivaliza con las trampas de sedimentos de aguas profundas.

Migración Vertical Diaria (DVM): La DVM crea un componente de bomba biológica activa: los organismos que se alimentan en la superficie, respiran y excretan a profundidad inyectan efectivamente carbono disuelto y particulado por debajo de la termoclina. Los patrones de DVM se desplazan con anomalías térmicas.

Exclusión Competitiva y la Paradoja del Plancton: La Hipótesis de Perturbación Intermedia formaliza que la diversidad máxima de especies ocurre a frecuencias de perturbación intermedias. Para GROAN: un arrecife con variabilidad de perturbación reducida puede paradójicamente perder biodiversidad incluso sin eventos agudos de blanqueamiento. El CMIE debe evaluar el régimen de perturbación como variable, no solo la intensidad del factor estresante.

Application
EN — English
  • 01When diagnosing suppressed NPP at a corridor reef site, apply Liebig's Law as the first-order filter before attributing the decline to temperature or light: measure N:P:Si ratios against Redfield baseline. If N:P is elevated above 16:1, anthropogenic nitrogen loading is indicated; route to Tier 2 local runoff intervention in DMAP-CAL™. If N:P is depressed, a nitrogen-cycling perturbation (fixation or denitrification pulse) is indicated; flag for GKIN analysis. Never apply nutrient supplementation interventions without confirming which nutrient is limiting — adding the non-limiting nutrient produces zero NPP recovery and wastes intervention resources.
  • 02When MOOP records a significant SST anomaly (+1°C or greater sustained for >4 weeks), apply the Sverdrup Hypothesis immediately to assess NPP risk before bleaching thresholds are reached. A positive SST anomaly strengthens thermal stratification, shallows the thermocline, suppresses nutrient entrainment from depth, and reduces the critical depth — potentially triggering NPP decline weeks before coral physiological bleaching response. Report this as a pre-bleaching productivity decline signal in the CMIE record: it is an early warning indicator, not a concurrent bleaching observation.
  • 03When assessing seagrass bed condition through GSIN observations, include a biological pump efficiency estimate: seagrass systems with intact sediment carbon burial (dark sediment, strong rhizome architecture, no bioturbation disturbance) are functioning as active pump enhancers. A GSIN site showing seagrass loss must be flagged in the CMIE record as a carbon flux degradation event — not only a habitat loss event — because the burial efficiency loss has consequences for local Ω aragonite through reduced organic carbon drawdown that extend beyond the meadow boundary.
  • 04When recording species richness and trophic structure data in GRIN observations, evaluate disturbance regime history alongside acute stressor records: a reef with low thermal stress history but low species richness may be experiencing chronic reduced variability — a Competitive Exclusion / Intermediate Disturbance signal that will not appear in standard bleaching alert products. Enter this as a CMIE disturbance regime flag. Conversely, a reef with moderate thermal stress history and high species richness may have benefited from intermediate disturbance dynamics — its resilience projection is higher than thermal exposure metrics alone would indicate.
  • 05When integrating GKIN kinetic index data with GRIN trophic structure data across a corridor transect, assess DVM contribution to trophic connectivity: sites with confirmed DVM populations (acoustic backscatter or net tow evidence from MOOP) and compressed vertical temperature gradients (<50 m thermocline depth) should be flagged for reduced active carbon transport and reduced trophic linkage between reef and midwater communities. This is an SST-mediated CMIE signal — thermal stratification → DVM compression → reduced active pump → trophic isolation of reef apex predators from midwater prey — that no single module can detect in isolation.
