▸ Lecture — Chemical Oceanography: Systems Overview
🇺🇸 English

Section 02's systems overview lesson integrates the chemical parameters introduced in L01 and the reaction mechanisms developed in L02 into the four major biogeochemical cycles operating simultaneously in every reef ecosystem: the carbon cycle, the nitrogen cycle, the phosphorus cycle, and the sulfur cycle. These cycles are not independent — they are chemically and biologically coupled, and their interactions determine whether a reef system is resilient or vulnerable. GROAN's CMIE is specifically designed to detect and weight these inter-cycle interactions, making biogeochemical systems literacy the chemical foundation of the PhD contribution.

The carbon cycle in reef systems operates across three coupled pathways. Photosynthetic carbon fixation by zooxanthellae and algae converts dissolved CO₂ into organic carbon — fueling the food web and drawing down local pCO₂. Calcification converts dissolved carbonate ion into CaCO₃ skeleton — simultaneously consuming alkalinity and releasing CO₂ (net: 2HCO₃⁻ → CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O), making reefs net carbon sources at the local scale. Respiration and decomposition by the entire biological community remineralize organic carbon back to CO₂, completing the internal cycle. The balance among these three pathways determines whether a reef's net metabolism is autotrophic (carbon-fixing) or heterotrophic (carbon-releasing) — a state variable that shifts under bleaching and stressor loading and that GROAN tracks through the GRIN productivity sub-index.

The nitrogen cycle in reef systems is the most complex of the four, involving five distinct biological transformation processes. Nitrogen fixation (N₂ → NH₄⁺) by cyanobacteria and diazotrophic bacteria provides the internal nitrogen source that sustains oligotrophic reef productivity. Nitrification (NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻) by nitrifying bacteria converts ammonium to nitrate — the form most readily assimilated by phytoplankton and algae. Denitrification (NO₃⁻ → N₂) by anaerobic bacteria in sediments removes fixed nitrogen from the system, regenerating N₂ gas and regulating the nitrogen budget. Assimilation by primary producers and remineralization by decomposers complete the internal loop. The nitrogen cycle is the chemical bridge between the physical stressor regime (freshwater input carrying DIN from land) and the biological stressor response (macroalgae bloom) — making it the most decision-relevant chemical cycle in the MAR context.

The phosphorus cycle lacks an atmospheric reservoir — unlike carbon and nitrogen, phosphorus has no gaseous phase. All phosphorus in the marine system originates from rock weathering, delivered via rivers and dust deposition. In reef systems, phosphorus is tightly retained by adsorption onto carbonate sediment surfaces — a geochemical property that buffers reef waters against phosphorus loading and explains why Caribbean reefs are more commonly nitrogen-limited than phosphorus-limited. However, when carbonate sediment surfaces become saturated (high riverine loading) or when resuspension releases sorbed phosphorus from bottom sediments, the buffering capacity is exceeded and phosphorus availability spikes — triggering algal growth in conjunction with available nitrogen. The Redfield Ratio (S02/L01) determines which nutrient is limiting at any given moment; GROAN's nutrient sub-index tracks both N and P concentrations to diagnose limitation state in real time.

The sulfur cycle is the least familiar of the four but carries a direct reef management relevance: dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP) — produced by zooxanthellae as an osmotic stress protectant — is the primary sulfur compound in reef systems. When bleaching occurs, DMSP is released from expelled zooxanthellae into the water column, where microbial degradation converts it to dimethylsulfide (DMS). DMS volatilizes to the atmosphere and has historically been proposed as a biogenic climate feedback mechanism (the CLAW hypothesis). More immediately relevant: DMSP is the settlement cue compound that coral planula larvae detect on CCA surfaces (introduced in S03/L02) — its concentration in reef water is therefore a proxy for both zooxanthellae health and larval settlement conditions, linking the sulfur cycle to coral reproduction and recovery.

Cycle coupling and CMIE integration: the four cycles are linked through shared reactants and biological mediators. Carbon and nitrogen are coupled through the Redfield Ratio — the C:N:P stoichiometry of phytoplankton growth simultaneously governs organic carbon production and nitrogen demand. Nitrogen and phosphorus cycles are coupled through co-limitation dynamics — the nutrient that is scarcer governs production rate. The sulfur cycle is coupled to carbon through DMSP-DMS production by zooxanthellae, which are in turn governed by carbon fixation rates. When a physical stressor (SST anomaly from S01) disrupts zooxanthellae (biological consequence from S03), it simultaneously alters carbon fixation rates, DMSP release, and nitrogen demand — triggering cascading chemical changes across all four cycles simultaneously. The CMIE detects this cascade through the convergence of signals from GRIN chemical sub-indices, GMAIN mangrove-lagoon chemistry, and GKIN fish assemblage responses, producing a Decision Score that reflects the full biogeochemical state of the system — not a single-cycle snapshot.

🇲🇽 Español

La lección de visión general de sistemas de la Sección 02 integra los cuatro ciclos biogeoquímicos principales que operan simultáneamente en cada ecosistema arrecifal: el ciclo del carbono, el ciclo del nitrógeno, el ciclo del fósforo y el ciclo del azufre. Estos ciclos no son independientes — están química y biológicamente acoplados, y sus interacciones determinan si un sistema arrecifal es resiliente o vulnerable.

El ciclo del carbono en los sistemas arrecifales opera a través de tres vías acopladas: la fijación fotosintética de carbono por zooxantelas y algas, la calcificación que convierte el ion carbonato en esqueleto de CaCO₃ (haciendo que los arrecifes sean fuentes netas de carbono a escala local), y la respiración y descomposición que remineraliza el carbono orgánico de vuelta a CO₂. El balance entre estas tres vías determina si el metabolismo neto del arrecife es autotrófico o heterotrófico.

El ciclo del nitrógeno en los sistemas arrecifales es el más complejo de los cuatro, involucrando cinco procesos de transformación biológica distintos: fijación de nitrógeno, nitrificación, desnitrificación, asimilación y remineralización. El ciclo del nitrógeno es el puente químico entre el régimen de estresores físicos (ingreso de agua dulce que lleva NID desde tierra) y la respuesta biológica al estresor (floración de macroalgas) — convirtiéndolo en el ciclo químico más relevante para la toma de decisiones en el contexto del MAR.

El ciclo del fósforo carece de reservorio atmosférico. En los sistemas arrecifales, el fósforo es retenido estrechamente por adsorción sobre las superficies de sedimento carbonatado — una propiedad geoquímica que amortigua las aguas arrecifales contra la carga de fósforo y explica por qué los arrecifes del Caribe están más comúnmente limitados por nitrógeno que por fósforo.

El ciclo del azufre lleva una relevancia directa para la gestión arrecifal: el dimetilsulfoniopropionato (DMSP) — producido por las zooxantelas — es la señal de asentamiento que las larvas planula de coral detectan en las superficies de CCA. Su concentración en el agua arrecifal es por tanto un proxy tanto de la salud de las zooxantelas como de las condiciones de asentamiento larval — vinculando el ciclo del azufre con la reproducción y recuperación del coral.

El acoplamiento de ciclos e integración del CMIE: cuando un estresor físico (anomalía de TSM) perturba las zooxantelas, simultáneamente altera las tasas de fijación de carbono, la liberación de DMSP y la demanda de nitrógeno — desencadenando cambios químicos en cascada en los cuatro ciclos simultáneamente. El CMIE detecta esta cascada a través de la convergencia de señales de los subíndices químicos de GRIN, produciendo una Puntuación de Decisión que refleja el estado biogeoquímico completo del sistema.

▸ Western Caribbean Application — MAR & Rim Run™ Circuit
🇺🇸 English
  • The MAR nitrogen cycle is in anthropogenic disruption: AGRRA water quality data from Belize and Honduras document dissolved inorganic nitrogen (DIN) concentrations 3–8× above oligotrophic baseline at sites within 5 km of populated coastlines — indicating that external nitrogen loading from sewage and agriculture has overwhelmed the denitrification buffering capacity of coastal sediments and is delivering excess fixed nitrogen directly to reef algae. This is the chemical confirmation of the macroalgae dominance trend observable in belt transect surveys, and the nutrient sub-index input that the GRIN module ingests.
  • The phosphorus adsorption mechanism provides a geochemical explanation for the spatial pattern of reef degradation in the MAR: sites adjacent to carbonate sand flats (high adsorption capacity) show less phosphorus loading impact than sites adjacent to river delta sediments (siliciclastic, low carbonate content, poor phosphorus adsorption) — meaning reef chemical vulnerability maps onto sediment geology, a spatial variable that GROAN's GRIN can encode as a site-level modifier coefficient in the nutrient sub-index calculation.
  • DMSP dynamics during Rim Run™ transit provide a reef health indicator accessible without laboratory equipment: post-bleaching reef sites have a characteristic sulfurous odor detectable at snorkel depth from mass DMSP release and DMS volatilization — a qualitative field indicator that flags elevated bleaching prevalence before belt transect data is compiled, providing an immediate triage signal for MOOP survey priority at each circuit node.
  • Coupled N-P cycle disruption at MAR river mouth sites: when wet-season riverine discharge delivers simultaneous DIN and turbidity pulses, the phosphorus adsorption buffering on carbonate sediments is overwhelmed at the same time that light availability drops from turbidity — creating a chemical-physical compound event where the nutrient that would drive algae growth (N) arrives simultaneously with the physical condition (turbidity) that suppresses the coral photosynthesis needed to outcompete it. The CMIE's multi-cycle signal integration detects this simultaneous N + turbidity event as a higher-risk compound signal than either stressor alone.
  • Carbon cycle state as a reef health proxy: the net metabolic balance of a reef (autotrophic vs. heterotrophic) is measurable from dawn-to-dusk dissolved oxygen and pH changes at a fixed anchor point — an upstream-to-downstream chemical gradient that Shamrocket can measure passively from anchor position using the overnight DO logger and pH strip protocol established in S02/L02. A reef with net daytime DO increase and pH rise is net autotrophic (healthy, carbon-fixing); a reef with flat or declining DO and pH through the day is net heterotrophic (stressed, carbon-releasing) — a whole-system chemical diagnosis from two measurements.
