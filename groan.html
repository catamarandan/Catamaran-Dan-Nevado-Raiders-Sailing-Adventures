What might continuing change mean?

Use the maps to form a testable explanation. Sustained ocean heat stress may increase coral bleaching risk. Lower rainfall together with drier soils and changing vegetation can motivate a closer look at drought stress. These are research questions, not automatic diagnoses or GROAN forecasts.

Case Study

Hurricane Gilbert, 1988.

The design basis for GROAN™'s storm surge measurement system is not a hypothetical storm. It is the worst storm this reef system has on record, and the one no network was in place to measure.

The Storm

Hurricane Gilbert struck the Mesoamerican Reef in September 1988. Its eye made landfall on the northeastern Yucatan Peninsula on September 14 as a Category 5 hurricane, directly over the Cancun and Cozumel reef corridor. After crossing the peninsula it re-entered the Gulf of Mexico, restrengthened, and made a second landfall at La Pesca, Tamaulipas, as a Category 3 on September 16.

185 mphPeak sustained winds
888 mbLowest Atlantic pressure at the time
500 miStorm diameter
15–20 ftSurge at Yucatan eye crossing

At 500 miles across, Gilbert did not hit one country. Its circulation reached from the Gulf coast of Mexico to the Bay Islands of Honduras, affecting every reef zone in the corridor at once. More than 300 people died across the affected countries, and damage was estimated at $2.98 billion (1988 USD).

The Measurement Gap

No network measured what the storm did at the reef. Scientists working in the aftermath reconstructed the damage from biological surveys conducted weeks later. They could observe the damage, but they could not measure the force that caused it.

They could see what Gilbert did to the reef. They could not measure what Gilbert did to the reef.

The Measurement System

The GROAN™ storm surge network is specified to resolve a Gilbert-class event from the Veracruz reef system in the Gulf of Mexico to the Bay Islands of Honduras, a corridor of roughly 1,800 km. It is designed to a Category 5 wind and wave load envelope and uses five pole and buoy types:

  • Type 1 — Land-based coastal pole. Shoreline, dock, or seawall at waterline. Water level, wind speed, and wind direction.
  • Type 2 — River mouth / estuary pole. Water level, wind, and a tidal influence flag.
  • Type 3 — Offshore reef-edge pole. Storm-hardened platform at the barrier reef crest. Adds current speed and direction.
  • Type 4 — Atoll perimeter pole. Storm-hardened platform on the windward and leeward edges of atolls. Adds current speed and direction.
  • Type 5 — Deep approach buoy. 50–80 km offshore on the storm approach track. Wave height, wind, and current, with early warning ahead of the surge.

Five pole and buoy types: 1 land-based coastal • 2 river mouth • 3 offshore reef-edge • 4 atoll perimeter • 5 deep approach buoy

Offshore poles matter because the surge that reached the coast was generated at the reef first. A land-based network records what arrives at the beach. It does not record what happens to the reef. From Data to Decisions

Collecting the data is the first step. GROAN then runs it through a sequential pipeline:

  1. Data ingestion and quality validation. Every reading passes a six-layer quality control framework. Flagged readings are quarantined, not deleted, and the audit record is kept.
  2. Storm signal isolation. Each water level reading is split into its predicted tidal component and the storm-driven surplus. Wind is processed in parallel to explain where surge amplifies, such as semi-enclosed bays.
  3. Force calculation at the reef. Surge, wind, and current data from offshore poles yield the physical loads on each reef zone: water column pressure, current force, wave energy, and orbital velocity at the reef surface.
  4. Physical Disturbance Score™. Force components are combined into a score for each reef zone, weighted by coral morphology and calibrated against the GROAN longitudinal baseline.
  5. Forward outputs. Per zone: structural damage probability by coral morphology, recovery trajectories at 6, 12, 24, and 60 months under three intervention scenarios, restoration asset survival, and an intervention priority ranking. These update as new pole data arrives.

What the Comparison Shows

  • The gap is real. NOAA's review documents sparse surface and ocean observations, and the network is designed to fill that gap at the coast and reef.
  • The network complements NOAA; it does not replace it. Aircraft and satellite tools determine a storm's track and intensity. GROAN depends on those forecasts and adds measurements where they are missing.
  • Short and long warnings serve different purposes. A 48-hour track forecast tells authorities where a storm is going. The buoys' 2–3 hour advance data tells them what is reaching the reef. Neither replaces the other.
  • Forecast skill is not yet proven for GROAN. NOAA's track errors were measured against observed storms. The GROAN outputs have not been tested that way.
Country Impact Analysis

What Each Country Could Do With It.

The network produces five kinds of output: live physical measurements, quality-controlled records, zone-level Physical Disturbance Scores™ (PDS™), forward outputs per zone, and a permanent, corridor-wide event record. The sections below describe how each country in the coverage zone could use them. Everything here assumes the network is built and the forecasting is validated, which has not yet happened.

Mexico

Quintana Roo

Beach closures and restoration

Direct eye-crossing zone with 44 stations. Pre-landfall data can guide beach, ferry and reef-tour decisions. The post-storm priority ranking can direct restoration to the reef sections that need it most.

Veracruz

First reef record

The Sistema Arrecifal Veracruzano has no offshore monitoring today. Zone A would produce the first physical record of what a Gulf re-entry does to those reefs, and give the port surge data for its operations.

Campeche Bank

Offshore operations

Type 3 offshore poles give oil and gas operators wind, wave and surge data at the platforms. In 1988 about 5,000 oil workers were evacuated, so this is a direct operational use.

Grijalva & Usumacinta

River flood planning

River-mouth poles show how far surge travels upriver, informing flood planning for inland communities.

Belize

Atolls

Measuring the rim

Turneffe, Lighthouse Reef and Glover’s are the most exposed points in Zone C. Type 4 perimeter poles measure what the atoll rims actually take, not what the coast receives. That evidence matters to the tourism and fishing economies that depend on the atolls.

Carrie Bow Caye

Scientific baseline

The Smithsonian research station is an existing reference site. Its biological records could be linked to a continuous physical record from the network.

Belize River

Flood warning

River-mouth poles track how surge moves inland, supporting flood warnings for Belize City and river communities.

Guatemala

Amatique Bay

Surge amplification

The bay’s enclosed shape drives water inland, so surge inside it can exceed heights on the open coast. Four coastal stations inside the bay would let Puerto Barrios and Santo Tomás plan for those higher levels.

Motagua & Rio Dulce

Basin flood planning

River-mouth data supports flood planning in the basins that Gilbert’s rain affected.

Zone D

Thinner coverage

With 15 stations, Guatemala’s zone is the most dependent on interpolation from neighboring stations. Local decisions should account for that before relying on the data.

Honduras & the Bay Islands

Utila · Roatán · Guanaja

Lead time for islands

Each island gets coastal and offshore stations. The Type 5 buoy northeast of Guanaja is the earliest warning point for storms approaching from the southeast. A 2–3 hour lead time matters to island communities that depend on a single road or ferry.

Cayos Cochinos · Río Plátano

Protected areas

Continuous physical records support closures, zoning and enforcement in the marine park and biosphere reserve.

Ulúa · Chamelecón

Flood history

Gilbert and Mitch both drove flooding through these river systems. River-mouth poles support flood planning there.

Across All Four Countries

  • Shared, comparable data. Each country now sees only its own coast. A corridor-wide record lets officials compare impacts and coordinate across borders, using the same evidence.
  • Damage maps within hours. After Gilbert, scientists reconstructed damage from surveys weeks later. A network gives a damage picture quickly, so divers and restoration teams reach the right reefs first.
  • Restoration budgeting. The 6–60 month recovery and restoration-survival outputs let agencies compare intervention scenarios before committing money.
  • Protected-area management. Parks and biosphere reserves get a continuous physical record to justify closures, zoning and enforcement.
  • Research baseline. The quality-controlled, permanent record supports long-term work by universities and research institutions in the region.

What Each Country Would Need in Place

  1. Data-sharing agreements. The countries must agree on who receives the data, who may publish it, and how it is used. Those agreements do not yet exist.
  2. Institutional roles. National agencies must own the alerts they act on. GROAN produces the outputs; governments make the response decisions.
  3. Local maintenance. Storm-hardened poles and buoys need servicing after every event. Who performs that work in each country is not yet defined.
  4. Accountability. GROAN is operated by a private Arizona LLC. A government relying on it for life-safety decisions would need contractual commitments, transparency about the models, and an independent review of the calibration.

What the Network Does Not Do

  • It does not map flooding. Surge at a station is not inundation on a street. Flood mapping needs elevation data, building and road layouts, and inundation modeling. None of those are part of the specified system.
  • It does not issue population-level warnings. The PDS™ scores describe physical force on reef zones, not risk to people.
  • It does not measure between stations. Values between poles are interpolated. In Zone D, with 15 stations across the whole zone, most of the coverage is interpolated.
  • It does not make evacuation decisions. Those belong to national and local emergency authorities. GROAN supplies inputs to them and does not replace them.
  • It is not validated for those uses. Forecasting and intervention effectiveness have not been validated.

Population-level use would require inundation mapping, validated forecasting, and integration with each country’s emergency management system. None of those is in place.

Limits

The PDS™ values and the post-storm map are modeled, not sensor readings. The model is calibrated against a single event, Hurricane Gilbert, and may not generalize to other storms. Forecasting and intervention effectiveness have not been validated, and the 2–3 hour warning figure comes from forward-speed arithmetic, not a demonstrated forecast. Zone D has the thinnest coverage.

Next Phase · Proposed

From Reef Data to Shore Impact.

The network measures what the water does at the coast and reef. It does not yet say which buildings and streets that water would reach. A proposed next phase would combine GROAN's measurements with terrain, exposure and damage data to estimate shore impact. None of this is part of the current specification, and none of it has been validated.

What the Combined System Would Need

  • Terrain. Bathymetry for the seafloor, elevation data for land (national lidar where available, since coarse global data is often too imprecise), and a consistent vertical datum across land and sea.
  • Exposure. Building footprints, roads, ports and critical facilities, plus population data from national statistics agencies.
  • A coupled surge and wave model. It would take the network's surge and wind readings as boundary conditions and calculate how water and waves move over the land, including runup.
  • Data assimilation. Each station's readings would correct the model as the storm develops, replacing simple interpolation between poles with the model's physics.
  • Damage curves. Depth-damage relationships for the region's building types, developed or adapted locally.
  • Calibration and validation. Tuning against Gilbert's recorded surge, and testing against other storms before any output is used for decisions.

The dashed contour lines mark ground at 1, 2 and 3 metres above sea level, so the flood edge can be read against known heights. The dots are stations. Orange dots are stations whose ground sits below the surge level, and gold dots are stations above it. Each station's reading is shown beside it. This is the core of the overlay: measured water levels are placed on the terrain to show where the surge is reaching.
  • Panel 3 — Transect. This is a vertical slice taken along one line running from the land (left) out to the sea (right). The olive ground shows the height of the terrain. The dashed gold line is the surge level at 4.0 m. Where the ground falls below that line on land, the blue fill shows the flood water above the ground. Where the ground dips below sea level, it shows the sea floor. The slice makes it easy to see that flooding depends on the ground height along the path, not just on the distance from the coast.

What the image shows, and what it does not: it shows how measured water levels, terrain and modeled flooding would be combined. It does not show real flooding, real building damage or a real forecast. The readings are placeholders, and the flood extent is a simple calculation from the surge level and ground height. A real model would add waves, runup, drainage, buildings and uncertainty ranges.

How Outputs Would Be Used

The outputs would go to national and local emergency authorities. They would support decisions about which areas to warn and where to send crews first. GROAN would not make evacuation decisions, and the outputs would carry uncertainty ranges so authorities can see how reliable each prediction is.

Limits

Elevation error can outweigh every other input, especially in low, flat areas. Local features such as seawalls, dunes and canals change flooding in ways coarse models miss. Shore predictions depend on the storm track, which GROAN does not forecast. A wrongly reassuring map is the most serious risk, so every output would carry uncertainty and disclaimers. What Comes Next

Beyond the Ocean — A Planetary Intelligence System.

GROAN™ begins in the marine environment because that is where the data gaps are sharpest, the cascade dynamics are best understood, and a 24-inch-draft catamaran can go where no research vessel can. The Bahamas and Caribbean work is Phase 1 of something larger.

The architecture was never designed to stop at the waterline. The same logic that connects a mangrove creek to a reef — cross-ecosystem signals, causal inference, unified state vectors, a decision engine that keeps the whole thing accountable — extends to every domain on Earth.

Land. Sea. Air. One intelligence layer. One governance framework that tells you what changed, why, and what the options are.

The long-horizon vision is a planetary intelligence system: unified state vectors across terrestrial, marine, and atmospheric domains; cross-domain cascade detection that follows a signal from a deforested watershed through a coastal chemistry anomaly to a reef bleaching event; and a governance layer with the same accountability standards applied at every level — source data, causal model, decision output.

Phase 1 — Now

Marine

Reef, seagrass, mangrove, water column, fisheries, chemistry, plankton, kelp, open ocean. Eleven intelligence modules. Field platform: Shamrocket.

Phase 2 — Near Term

Coastal Integration

Terrestrial watersheds, river systems, and atmospheric deposition as upstream drivers of the marine signals GROAN already monitors. The cascade begins on land.

Phase 3 — Long Horizon

Planetary

Land, sea, and air — unified. Cross-domain state vectors. A decision engine that can reason across ecosystem boundaries that no single-domain monitoring program can cross.

Status

The planetary expansion is a design horizon, not a funded program. The marine layer is where GROAN™ is being built and validated first. The architecture is designed to extend — when the science, the data infrastructure, and the partnerships are ready.
